\chapter{Theoretical Background}\label{chap:theory}
\section{Sheet Metal Formability}
Sheet metal formability describes the ability of a sheet material to undergo plastic deformation during a forming operation without the occurrence of defects such as localized necking, fracture, wrinkling, or excessive thinning. It is a key consideration in the design of sheet metal components and forming processes, particularly in industries such as automotive, aerospace, and consumer goods manufacturing, where lightweight structures and complex geometries are increasingly required.

The formability of a sheet metal depends on a combination of material, geometrical, and process-related factors. Material properties such as yield strength, strain hardening behaviour, strain-rate sensitivity, anisotropy, and ductility directly influence the material's ability to accommodate plastic deformation. In addition, sheet thickness, tooling geometry, lubrication conditions, and the applied loading path can significantly affect the deformation behaviour and the onset of failure.

During a forming operation, deformation is rarely uniaxial and is generally characterized by a combination of major and minor in-plane strains. Different strain paths can lead to significantly different forming limits, even for the same material. Consequently, the characterization of sheet metal formability requires an approach capable of accounting for a wide range of deformation states.

Several methods have been developed to assess formability, including tensile tests, bulge tests, and standardized forming experiments. Among these, the Forming Limit Diagram (FLD) has become the most widely used engineering tool for describing the onset of localized necking under different strain paths. The FLD provides a graphical representation of the strain combinations that a material can withstand before instability occurs and forms the basis for the evaluation of sheet metal formability in both experimental and numerical investigations.

\section{Forming Limit Diagram}

The Forming Limit Diagram (FLD) is the most widely used tool for characterizing the formability of sheet metals. It plots the major principal strain, $\varepsilon_1$, against the minor principal strain, $\varepsilon_2$, at the onset of localized necking. The resulting Forming Limit Curve (FLC) defines the boundary between stable and unstable deformation. Strain states below the FLC correspond to safe deformation, whereas strain states above the curve indicate the onset of localized necking and, ultimately, fracture.

Figure~\ref{fig:FLD_example} illustrates a typical FLD and the corresponding deformation regions. The shape and position of the FLC depend on several factors, including material properties, sheet thickness, strain rate, temperature, and loading path. Three characteristic regions can be distinguished:

\begin{itemize}
    \item $\varepsilon_2 < 0$: uniaxial to plane-strain tension,
    \item $\varepsilon_2 = 0$: plane-strain condition, typically corresponding to the minimum of the FLC and therefore the most critical strain state,
    \item $\varepsilon_2 > 0$: biaxial stretching up to equi-biaxial tension.
\end{itemize}

\section{Nakazima and Marciniak Tests}
The Nakazima and Marciniak tests are the two most commonly used experimental methods for determining Forming Limit Curves (FLCs). Both tests aim to generate different strain paths by varying the specimen geometry, thereby covering the range from uniaxial tension to equi-biaxial strains required for constructing a complete Forming Limit Diagram (FLD).

In the Nakazima test, a hemispherical punch is driven into a clamped sheet specimen until localized necking or fracture occurs. Different strain paths are obtained by varying the specimen width, with narrow specimens approaching uniaxial tension and wider specimens approaching equi-biaxial stretching. Due to the direct contact between the punch and the specimen, the deformation is influenced by bending and contact pressure effects, which can lead to slightly non-linear strain paths at the specimen apex~\cite{ISO120042}.

The Marciniak test employs a flat-bottomed cylindrical punch together with a carrier blank positioned between the punch and the specimen. This arrangement prevents direct contact between the punch and the investigated sheet and promotes failure in a region of more homogeneous deformation. As a result, the Marciniak configuration generally produces more linear strain paths than the Nakazima test and is often considered more suitable for fundamental investigations of forming limits~\cite{ISO120042, Min2017}.

While ISO~12004-2 specifies a reference punch diameter of \SI{100}{mm} for both testing methods, the present work additionally investigates the influence of punch geometry by considering hemispherical punch diameters of \SI{40}{mm}, \SI{60}{mm}, \SI{80}{mm}, and \SI{100}{mm} in the Nakazima configuration. This enables a systematic assessment of the influence of punch size on the measured forming limits and contributes to the broader investigation of size effects in sheet metal forming.


\section{Punch-in-Punch Test}

The Punch-in-Punch (PiP) test is an alternative forming test designed to generate more linear strain paths than the standard Nakazima configuration, particularly in the biaxial stretching regime. It employs two coaxial punches acting sequentially on the specimen:

The process consists of two steps. In Step~1, both punches advance together, pre-forming the specimen. In Step~2, the outer punch is held fixed while the inner one advances further, deepening the dome until fracture. This two-stage loading produces a more controlled strain path.

\section{Forming Limit Identification Methods}

Determining the onset of localised necking from full-field strain data is non-trivial, as the transition from diffuse to localised necking is gradual and its detection depends on the chosen criterion. Several methods have been proposed in the literature, which can be broadly classified into position-dependent and time-dependent approaches. Position-dependent methods evaluate the spatial strain distribution at a single time instant, whereas time-dependent methods track the temporal evolution of the strain field and detect necking from changes in the strain rate. The following subsections describe the methods considered in this work.

\subsection{Intersection Line Method (DIN EN ISO 12004-2)}
\label{sec:din_method}

The intersection line method specified in ISO~12004-2~\cite{ISO120042} is a position-dependent approach that evaluates the spatial strain distribution on the last DIC frame recorded before fracture. Five parallel cross-sections (intersection lines) are constructed perpendicular to the crack, spaced 2\,mm apart, each extending 20\,mm on both sides of the fracture line. The orientation of the intersection lines depends on the minor strain at the crack position: for $\varepsilon_2 < 0$ (left side of the FLD), the lines run parallel to the specimen edges, whereas for $\varepsilon_2 > 0$, they are oriented perpendicular to the crack line.

Along each intersection line, the crack position is first determined by fitting a bell-shaped curve to the major strain profile $\varepsilon_1$ within a 4\,mm window around the strain maximum. The peak of this fit defines the precise crack location. Two fitting regions are then defined on either side of the crack. The interior boundary of each region is placed at 3\,mm from the crack, and the exterior boundary is set by the fitting window width:
\begin{equation}
    \omega = 10 \left[1 + \frac{\bar{\varepsilon}_2}{\bar{\varepsilon}_1}\right],
    \label{eq:din_window}
\end{equation}
where $\bar{\varepsilon}_1$ and $\bar{\varepsilon}_2$ are the average principal strains at the interior boundaries of both sides. The intersection-line spacing (2\,mm) and the constant factor of $10$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:din_window} are calibrated for the standard Nakazima geometry with a $\varnothing$100\,mm hemispherical punch. An inverse parabola is fitted over each region, and the value of the fit evaluated at the crack position yields the major necking strain $\varepsilon_{1,\text{neck}}$. The minor necking strain is obtained from the thickness strain:
\begin{equation}
    \varepsilon_3 = -(\varepsilon_1 + \varepsilon_2), \qquad
    \varepsilon_{2,\text{neck}} = -(\varepsilon_{3,\text{neck}} + \varepsilon_{1,\text{neck}}).
\end{equation}

The method is straightforward and widely used, but relies on a single time frame and is sensitive to the selected cross-section and the regularity of the strain field near the crack.

\subsection{Volk & Hora Time-Dependent Method}
\label{sec:vh_method}

In contrast to the intersection-line method, the approach proposed by
Volk and Hora~\cite{VolkHora2011} is a time-dependent method that identifies
the onset of localized necking from the evolution of the thickness strain rate.
First, the thickness strain rate is computed from the in-plane DIC strains
using plastic incompressibility:
\begin{equation}
\dot{\varepsilon}_3 =
-\left(\dot{\varepsilon}_1 + \dot{\varepsilon}2\right).
\end{equation}
For discrete time steps, the central difference formula is preferred because
of its higher accuracy. If the frame rate changes during the experiment, the
effective time step is defined as
$\Delta t_n = \min(\Delta t_n^{-}, \Delta t_n^{+})$, and virtual strain values
are linearly interpolated to ensure a uniform differencing stencil. The
thinning rate is then defined as
$\dot{\varepsilon}{\text{thin}} = -\dot{\varepsilon}_3$.

A necking zone is identified from the thinning-rate field following the
procedure of Volk and Hora~\cite{VolkHora2011}. On the last frame before
fracture, denoted by $b-1$, the DIC facets are sorted by their thinning rate in
ascending order. The representative maximum thinning rate is defined as the
mean of the five highest values:
\begin{equation}
\bar{\dot{\varepsilon}}{\max}
= \frac{1}{5}\sum{i=g-4}^{g} \dot{\varepsilon}{b-1,i},
\end{equation}
where $g$ is the total number of facets and the subscript $b-1$ indicates that
the thinning rate is evaluated at the last frame before fracture. The necking
zone $N$ is then defined as all facets whose thinning rate exceeds a fraction
$\alpha$ of this representative maximum:
\begin{equation}
N =
\left{
i ; \middle| ;
\dot{\varepsilon}{b-1,i}
\geq \alpha , \bar{\dot{\varepsilon}}_{\max}
\right},
\end{equation}
where $\alpha$ is selected such that a sufficient number of facets is included
in the necking zone. For a grid size of approximately \SI{1}{\milli\meter}, a
typical value is $\alpha = 0.55$.

The mean thinning rate inside the necking zone is then plotted as a function of
time. Two linear fits are applied: one to the stable pre-necking regime and one
to the unstable post-necking regime. The transition frame is determined by
minimising the combined fitting error of both linear regressions. This frame is
interpreted as the last stable frame before localized necking. The limit strains
are finally computed as the mean principal strains in the necking zone at this
frame:
\begin{equation}
\varepsilon_{1,\text{lim}}
= \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \varepsilon_{1,\text{stab},i},
\qquad
\varepsilon_{2,\text{lim}}
= \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \varepsilon_{2,\text{stab},i},
\end{equation}
where $n$ is the number of facets in the necking zone.

Figure~\ref{fig:vh_example} shows an example of the resulting Volk–Hora
evaluation plot obtained with the implemented post-processing framework. The
change in slope of the mean thinning-rate curve marks the transition from the
stable to the unstable regime and is used to identify the limit frame.

\subsection{Merklein Method}

The Merklein method~\cite{Merklein2010} is a time-dependent approach that detects necking from the acceleration of the major strain rate at a critical point. On the last frame before fracture, the facet with the highest major strain value within the necking zone is selected. To reduce scatter, the mean major strain within a circle of 2\,mm radius around this point is tracked over time.

During Nakazima tests, the major strain rate increases approximately linearly until the transition from elastic to plastic deformation, after which a mostly linear progress of the strain rate is observed until necking begins. A drastic increase in the time derivative of the strain rate is observed during the final stages before fracture. To objectively determine the necking time point, a regression curve of the coefficient of determination ($R^2$) is computed for all data points up to each candidate time. The maximum of this regression curve indicates the onset of necking. The limit strains are the mean values of major and minor strains within the 2\,mm radius at the identified necking time.

\subsection{Sigvant Method}

The Sigvant method~\cite{Pham2023} detects necking using the coefficient of variation (CoV) of the major strain rate within a local region of interest. The CoV is defined as:
\begin{equation}
    \text{CoV} = \frac{\sigma}{\mu},
\end{equation}
where $\mu$ and $\sigma$ are the mean and standard deviation of the major strain rate within a circle of 5\,mm diameter centred on the point of highest equivalent plastic strain on the last frame before fracture.

During homogeneous deformation, the mean strain rate increases steadily while the standard deviation remains approximately constant, causing the CoV to decrease. Once localisation begins, the standard deviation increases sharply, leading to a rapid rise in the CoV. The global minimum of the CoV therefore identifies the onset of necking. The limit strains are the mean principal strains in the region of interest at the frame corresponding to this minimum.

\subsection{Min–Stoughton Curvature Method}
\label{sec:minstoughton_method}

The forming-limit curve is traditionally interpreted as a fixed material
boundary in the $\varepsilon_1$--$\varepsilon_2$ plane. This interpretation is
strictly valid only for proportional loading, since the strain combination at
the onset of necking depends on the path followed to reach it. In the Nakazima
test the contact with the hemispherical punch bends the sheet and curves the
strain path, so a limit point extracted under the assumption of a linear path
no longer corresponds to a unique material limit. A criterion that detects
necking directly, from a physical signature of localization rather than from
the position of the strain state, is therefore desirable.

To address this need, Min et al.~\cite{Min2017} proposed an improved curvature
method that detects the onset of localized necking from the evolution of the
specimen surface geometry rather than from the strain state. The method was
first developed for the Marciniak test, in which a carrier blank produces a
nearly flat and almost proportionally loaded region, and was then extended to
the Nakazima test, where the hemispherical punch bends the sheet and imposes a
strong global curvature. In both configurations the criterion is spatio-temporal.
It evaluates how a local geometrical imperfection develops in space and how its
curvature evolves over time, which makes the detection largely independent of
the strain path.

The underlying idea is illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:min_stoughton_principle}.
During homogeneous deformation, the outer surface of the specimen remains
smooth and its curvature evolves gradually. When localized necking starts, a
small dimple forms in the necking region. This dimple introduces an additional
local curvature superimposed on the global curvature of the dome. By tracking
this local curvature over the deformation sequence, the onset of localized
necking can be identified.

The curvature is not evaluated over the whole surface but on a small point
matrix placed around the necking region, as shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:ms_point_matrix}. The matrix is centred on the neck centre $O$
and oriented along the band, with a width $W_X$ across the band and a length
$W_Y$ along it, sampled by $N_X$ columns of $N_Y$ points. For the Nakazima test,
where the sheet wraps around the punch, each surface point is expressed in
punch-based coordinates: an arc-distance coordinate $D$ measured along a matrix
column and a radial coordinate $R_\mathrm{out}$ giving the distance from the
point to the punch centre $O'$. These coordinates separate the local necking
signal from the global curvature imposed by the punch.

To isolate the local necking contribution, the background curvature of the
specimen is first removed from the measured surface profile by referring it to
an earlier, still homogeneous state. In the Marciniak test this background
component is small, because the analysed region remains nearly flat, whereas in
the Nakazima test it is dominated by the dome shape imposed by the hemispherical
punch and must therefore be treated explicitly. After this subtraction, the
remaining residual profile contains the local geometrical deviation caused by
necking. A circle is then fitted to the residual profile around the
localization region. If the fitted circle has radius $\rho$, the corresponding
local curvature is defined as
\begin{equation}
C_{pm} = \frac{1}{\rho}.
\end{equation}

    dimple associated with necking. A circle is fitted to the residual profile

    \label{fig:min_stoughton_principle}
\end{figure}


A nearly flat residual profile results in a large radius and therefore a small
curvature, whereas a developing dimple produces a smaller radius and a larger
curvature.

The curvature value $C_{pm}$ is evaluated for each frame of the deformation
sequence. During stable deformation, the curvature follows a smooth evolution.
At the onset of localized necking, the measured curvature starts to increase
more rapidly and departs from its previous trend. The measured curvature is
therefore compared with a predicted value $C_{pm,P}$ obtained by extrapolating
the earlier curvature evolution. Localized necking is detected at the first
frame $k$ for which
\begin{equation}
C_{pm}(k) > C_{pm,P}(k) + \Delta
\end{equation}
is satisfied over a prescribed number of consecutive frames. The tolerance
$\Delta$ and the persistence requirement are introduced to avoid detecting
isolated fluctuations caused by measurement noise. This comparison against a
predicted curvature, combined with the persistence requirement, is the main
refinement that Min et al.~\cite{Min2017} introduce over earlier curvature-based
detection, and it is what makes the method robust enough to be applied to both
Marciniak and Nakazima tests.

Once the onset frame has been identified from the curvature evolution, the
corresponding forming-limit strains are extracted from the DIC strain field at
the localization centre. The curvature criterion is therefore used to determine
the time of onset, while the limit strain values are still obtained from the
measured strain data.
