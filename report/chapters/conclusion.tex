\chapter{Conclusion}\label{chap:conclusion}

This report addressed the three connected tasks of the project \textit{Towards
Robust Forming Limit Curve Identification: Experiments and Modeling}. Task~I
generated the experimental forming-limit data used to study material
orientation, sheet thickness, punch diameter, and evaluation-window effects.
Task~II extended the identification workflow by examining a Min--Stoughton
curvature-based criterion. Task~III supplied an automated numerical framework
for generating controlled simulation data for the same specimen and punch
configurations.

The experimental campaign compared the rolling and transverse directions for
every specimen geometry and sheet thickness with the standard
\SI{100}{\milli\meter} punch. The RD and TD mean forming-limit
points differ by no more than \num{0.019} in major and \num{0.017} in minor
strain across all seven widths and four thicknesses, of the same order as the
per-geometry repeat scatter, with the largest difference at the equi-biaxial
\texttt{W200} point of the thickest sheet~D. No width or thickness showed a systematic
orientation-dependent shift, so the RD and TD forming limit curves are
indistinguishable within the repeatability of the campaign, and a single
orientation-independent reference is justified for this material. The
punch-diameter study showed that applying the standard ISO~12004-2 window
unchanged to nonstandard punch diameters can distort the extracted limit,
especially for the \SI{40}{\milli\meter} punch, whose curve breaks the
otherwise continuous size trend. The geometry-dependent $D/10$ factor is a
simple candidate that links the fitting window to the punch diameter and
restores a consistent ordering of the FLCs across punch sizes for this
campaign, although a dedicated calibration of the window remains open.

The Min--Stoughton study demonstrated that the curvature workflow is
operational on laboratory DIC exports and can produce onset frames in the range
of the ISO~12004-2 and Volk--Hora results. However, the sensitivity to matrix
length, reference-frame choice, and DIC coverage means that the method is not
yet calibrated for production FLC extraction.

On the numerical side, a reproducible finite element analysis (FEA) pipeline
now covers the full chain from automated model generation through
\software{SLURM} execution to post-processing of the \software{Abaqus}
output database. The sensitivity studies fixed a verified working
configuration at $\Delta t_\mathrm{MS}=\SI{1e-6}{\second}$ and
$f_\mathrm{MR}=1.41$, which is quasi-static by the energy criterion and
gives a fracture strain within \SI{1.9}{\percent} of the finest tested mesh
at \SI{63}{\percent} of its run time. The friction check
showed that only frictionless punch contact keeps the fracture dome-centred,
while any nonzero friction moves the failure site off the dome and the
strain path toward plane strain, which supports the frictionless baseline
for the lubricated experiments. The fracture-based extraction already yields
an exploratory fracture forming limit curve (FFLC), and a necking-based FLC
comparable with the ISO evaluation awaits a robust onset detection.

In summary, the report connects the experimental, data-evaluation, and
simulation parts of the project into one workflow. The remaining work is
mainly quantitative: calibrating the punch friction and the AA5754 material
constants, running the width campaign with validation against the measured
force responses and failure locations, and hardening the necking-onset
detection so that the campaign yields a necking-based FLC rather than only a
fracture curve.
