\chapter{Results}
\label{chap:results}

\section{Task I: Experimental Results}
\label{sec:results_task1}
This section reports the main experimental observations obtained from the
forming test campaign. The focus is placed on the influence of material
orientation, punch diameter, and sheet thickness on the experimentally
identified forming limits.


\subsection{Influence of Material Orientation}
\label{sec:orientation_influence}

A dedicated subset of the campaign was used to isolate the influence of
material orientation on the experimentally determined forming limits. For
every sheet thickness (A--D), the standard Nakazima test was performed with
specimens oriented in the rolling direction (RD, configuration
\texttt{E0\_RM00\_000}) and in the transverse direction (TD, configuration
\texttt{E0\_RM00\_090}). The suffix denotes the orientation
angle $\alpha$ relative to RD, with \SI{0}{\degree} for RD and
\SI{90}{\degree} for TD. The punch diameter, specimen widths, and test-bench
settings were kept identical between the two orientations, so that
differences in the resulting forming limits can be interpreted primarily as
orientation effects.

For each orientation and thickness, two complete campaigns of seven specimen
widths were performed, and the forming-limit points reported here are the mean
over the two campaigns, which reduces the influence of test specific scatter.
The limit points were determined from
the DIC strain fields using the ISO~12004-2 intersection-line method
described in Section~\ref{sec:din_method}. Figure~\ref{fig:rd_vs_td} overlays the resulting RD and TD forming
limit curves for the four thicknesses.

Across all four thicknesses, the RD and TD forming limit curves are nearly
coincident, following the same trajectory from the uniaxial strain-state through the
plane-strain minimum and into the biaxial strain-state. The differences in
Table~\ref{tab:rd_td_summary} remain within $\pm0.019$ in $\varepsilon_1$ and
$\pm0.017$ in $\varepsilon_2$. The largest differences
occur at the equi-biaxial \texttt{W200} point of the thickest sheet~D, where the transverse case has only one usable repeat. Without a second repetition this point retains the full test dispersion that averaging removes everywhere else, so part of its larger difference is attributable to test to test variation rather than to orientation. 

These results indicate that, for the AA5754 sheet material and thickness range
investigated here, in-plane material orientation produces no systematic shift in
the forming limits beyond the repeat scatter of the campaign. This justifies
restricting the remaining punch-diameter tests to the rolling direction and
supports using one orientation-independent experimental FLC reference for this
dataset.

\subsection{Punch Diameter, Sheet Thickness, and the Evaluation Window}
\label{sec:punch_thickness}

The second experimental study varies the punch diameter
($\varnothing$\SI{40}{\milli\meter}, $\varnothing$\SI{60}{\milli\meter},
$\varnothing$\SI{80}{\milli\meter}, and
$\varnothing$\SI{100}{\milli\meter}) across the four sheet thicknesses (A--D)
to expose the coupling between punch geometry and sheet thickness. Physically,
a smaller punch superimposes more bending on the membrane stretch, since
wrapping a sheet of thickness $t$ around a punch of radius $R$ adds a bending
strain of about $t/2R$ between the outer surface and the mid plane. This
through thickness strain gradient, together with the contact pressure of the
punch, stabilises the deformation against localised necking and raises the
limit strains measured on the outer
surface~\cite{Charpentier1975, Min2016}. Both effects scale with the ratio
$t/R$. Two trends are therefore expected: the FLC should rise
as the punch diameter decreases, and the spread between punch diameters
should widen with increasing thickness, since $t/R$ grows fastest for the
smallest punch on the thickest sheet.

Figure~\ref{fig:flc_norm} shows the forming limit curves obtained with the
standard ISO~12004-2 evaluation, using the window width of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:din_window} with its calibration constant of $10$. The
\SI{100}{\milli\meter}, \SI{80}{\milli\meter}, and \SI{60}{\milli\meter}
punches form a closely spaced group, but the diameter ordering is not preserved
throughout the ISO result. The \SI{40}{\milli\meter} punch does not show a
consistent upward shift relative to the larger punches. Instead, parts of its
FLC are lower than the larger-punch group, and the sequence of the curves
changes with sheet thickness and strain state. This behaviour is not
compatible with a simple curvature driven size effect, which would be expected
to vary continuously with punch diameter. It points to an interaction between
the fixed ISO fitting window and the neck geometry of the nonstandard punches.
The standard window is calibrated for the
$\varnothing$\SI{100}{\milli\meter} Nakazima geometry, whereas the
\SI{40}{\milli\meter} punch produces a much narrower neck, so a window sized
for the standard punch no longer matches the strain field to which it is
applied.

The intersection-line method contains two length scales. The first is the
spacing between neighbouring intersection lines, which remains fixed at
\SI{2}{\milli\meter} throughout this study. The second is the neck-window width
used for the ISO parabola fit, introduced through the constant $10$ in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:din_window}. Only this second parameter is varied in the tuning
below. The line spacing controls how densely the strain field is sampled near
the crack path, whereas the fitting window controls how much of the neck
profile is used to identify the limit strain. The fitting window is expected
to depend on punch size because the strained dome and neck profile become
narrower when the punch diameter $D$ decreases. A constant calibrated on the
$\varnothing$\SI{100}{\milli\meter} ISO punch is therefore not necessarily
transferable to the \SI{80}{\milli\meter}, \SI{60}{\milli\meter}, and
\SI{40}{\milli\meter} punches.

As a diagnostic, the window sensitivity is first checked with deliberately
smaller fixed constants. Figure~\ref{fig:flc_wvar} compares constants 10, 9, 8,
and 7. This is not a recalibration, but a way to test whether a reduced window
moves the curves towards a more consistent punch-diameter ordering. The
observed shift motivates a simple geometry-scaled candidate, replacing the
fixed constant by $D/10$. The window width then becomes
$\omega = (D/10)\,[1 + \bar{\varepsilon}_2/\bar{\varepsilon}_1]$, which
preserves the standard value for the $\varnothing$\SI{100}{\milli\meter} punch
and reduces the window for the smaller punches. Figure~\ref{fig:flc_pscaled}
shows the corresponding re-evaluation.

These observations indicate that the ISO~12004-2 evaluation, calibrated for a
single standardised punch, cannot be transferred unchanged to a campaign that
varies the punch diameter. The $D/10$ rule is a first,
deliberately simple candidate because it links the fitted neck window to the
punch diameter while preserving the standard value for the
$\varnothing$\SI{100}{\milli\meter} punch. The intersection-line spacing was
not changed in this study. Since smaller punches may also create a thinner
neck region, the fixed \SI{2}{\milli\meter} spacing should be checked in a separate sensitivity study.

\section{Task II: Min--Stoughton Curvature Criterion}
\label{sec:results_task2}

The standalone Min--Stoughton Nakazima workflow described in
Section~\ref{sec:minstoughton_workflow_assessment} was applied to the complete
rolling-direction campaign of all four sheet thicknesses, A
($t=\SI{1.2}{\milli\meter}$), B ($t=\SI{1.5}{\milli\meter}$), C
($t=\SI{2.0}{\milli\meter}$), and D ($t=\SI{3.0}{\milli\meter}$), with the
\SI{100}{\milli\meter} punch. Each thickness provides the seven Nakazima widths
from \texttt{W020} to \texttt{W200} in two repetitions, so the data set contains
\num{56} DIC samples. The runs were executed
from the \software{VIC-3D} exports, with the specimen metadata resolved as
described in Table~\ref{tab:ms_constants}. 

\subsection{Baseline Full-Campaign Evaluation}
\label{sec:ms_campaign}

As a baseline, the curvature criterion was applied to the full campaign with a
fixed parameter set taken from a first calibration sweep:
$W_Y=\SI{15}{\milli\meter}$, $M=0.8$, $n=8$,
$\mathrm{SAC}=\SI{5e-4}{\per\milli\meter}$, and $\alpha=0.1$. The result was
compared, for every thickness, against the ISO~12004-2 and Volk--Hora limits
extracted from the same DIC samples. Figure~\ref{fig:flc_ms_comparison}
overlays the three criteria in the forming-limit diagram for the four
thicknesses, Table~\ref{tab:ms_campaign_summary} summarises the detection rate
and the mean $|\Delta\varepsilon_1|$ and $|\Delta\varepsilon_2|$ components
relative to ISO~12004-2 per thickness, and the width-by-width repeat values are
tabulated in Appendix~\ref{app:ms_campaign_tables}.

As Figure~\ref{fig:flc_ms_comparison} shows, the curvature criterion detects an
onset in 13 of 14 samples for both class~A and class~B, 12 of 14 for class~C,
and all 14 for class~D. For the thinner sheets, the curvature limit stays close
to the established methods: class~B gives mean absolute differences of
\num{0.014} in $\varepsilon_1$ and \num{0.009} in $\varepsilon_2$, compared
with \num{0.024} and \num{0.007} for class~A. The $\varepsilon_1$ component
increases for the thicker sheets, reaching \num{0.033} for class~C and
\num{0.046} for class~D. The $\varepsilon_2$ component remains smaller, with
mean values of \num{0.013} and \num{0.016}. Class~D shows a negative bias, with
the curvature point falling below both reference methods at most widths. For
example, the \texttt{W020} point gives $\varepsilon_1=\num{0.285}$ against the
ISO~12004-2 value of \num{0.358}. This baseline from the first calibration sweep
does not describe all thicknesses equally well, which motivates the broader
parameter sweep below.

\subsection{Parameter Sweep Across Thicknesses}
\label{sec:ms_sweep_thickness}

To test whether the baseline underestimation is parameter dependent rather
than intrinsic to the curvature criterion, a parameter sweep was run over all
four thicknesses (\num{56} samples), covering
$W_Y\in\{15,20,25\}\,\si{\milli\meter}$,
$M\in\{0.70,0.75,0.80\}$, $n\in\{6,8,10\}$,
$\mathrm{SAC}\in\{\num{5e-4},\num{1e-3}\}\,\si{\per\milli\meter}$, and
$\alpha\in\{0.10,0.20\}$, that is \num{108} parameter sets per sample. The Euler
sweep output stores the detected limit strains for each sample, so two rankings
are kept separate. The detection rank maximises the number of detected onsets
and uses the strain errors only to order ties. The ISO-match rank is evaluated
only for parameter sets detecting at least 13 of 14 samples and is ordered by
the mean $|\Delta\varepsilon_1|$ relative to ISO~12004-2. The corresponding
$|\Delta\varepsilon_2|$ value is reported next to it rather than hidden in a
single distance. The complete compact $|\Delta\varepsilon_1|$ sweep is tabulated
in Appendix~\ref{app:ms_sweep}.

still be read as parameter screening because the same samples are used for
ranking and comparison.

\section{Task III: Numerical Framework Verification Status}
\label{sec:numerical_results}

This section reports the verification status of the numerical framework, not a
completed numerical FLC campaign. Since the material constants are the AA5182
surrogate of Chapter~\ref{chap:numerical_model}, the runs are used to check the
workflow and numerical settings rather than to make quantitative predictions
for the AA5754 experiments. The configuration parameters are listed in
Appendix~\ref{app:configuration_parameters}.

\subsection{Sensitivity Study}
\label{sec:sensitivity_study}

The sensitivity study uses the Nakazima \texttt{W100} specimen with the
\SI{100}{\milli\meter} punch as one reference case. The first batch varies the
mesh-refinement factor and the mass-scaling target separately. The order below
follows the logic of the checks. The mesh sweep at
$\Delta t_\mathrm{MS}=\SI{1e-5}{\second}$ is treated as an exploratory
screening run, the mass-scaling sweep then fixes the conservative target, and a
final mesh check repeats the refinement study at this target. Energy ratios are
kept as full-history plots and compact table checks, since fracture and element
deletion can create kinetic-energy spikes that should not be read as stable
forming-window behaviour.

\subsubsection{Mesh Refinement Analysis}
\label{sec:mesh_refinement_analysis}

The screening sweep keeps $\Delta t_\mathrm{MS}=\SI{1e-5}{\second}$ fixed and
varies the refinement factor of Section~\ref{sec:mesh_generation} over
$f_\mathrm{MR}=1$, $2$, and $3$. The strain paths in
Figure~\ref{fig:mr_sweep_results} remain close for most of the process. The
extracted fracture strains in Table~\ref{tab:mesh_refinement_walltime} do not,
since $\varepsilon_1^\mathrm{f}$ varies non-monotonically between \num{0.210}
and \num{0.254} while $\varepsilon_2^\mathrm{f}$ stays at \num{0.098}. In
addition, the finest mesh exceeds the \SI{5}{\percent} energy guideline in the
zoomed panel of Figure~\ref{fig:mr_sweep_results}, because the fixed target
increment scales the most mass onto the smallest elements. Mesh convergence is
therefore not obtained from this sweep, and the final mesh is only selected
after the mass-scaling target is fixed.

\subsubsection{Mass Scaling Analysis}
\label{sec:mass_scaling_analysis}

The mass-scaling sweep varies the target time increment while keeping the mesh
refinement factor fixed at $f_\mathrm{MR}=2$. The evaluated target increments are
$1\times10^{-6}$, $5\times10^{-6}$, $1\times10^{-5}$, $5\times10^{-5}$, and
$1\times10^{-4}$\,\si{\second}.

Figure~\ref{fig:ms_sweep_results} shows that the strain paths are similar for
the smaller target increments but separate for the aggressive mass-scaling
settings. In the energy-ratio history, the first sharp increase occurs when the
punch first contacts the blank. The second spike is associated with fracture
and element deletion. The zoom around the \SI{5}{\percent} guideline therefore
has to be read between these two events. Only
$\Delta t_\mathrm{MS}=\SI{1e-6}{\second}$ provides a sufficiently long forming
interval below \SI{5}{\percent}. The larger target increments either remain too
dynamic or drop below the criterion too late.

\subsubsection{Working Numerical Parameters}
\label{sec:selection_numerical_parameters}

After the mass-scaling sweep, the mesh check is repeated at
$\Delta t_\mathrm{MS}=\SI{1e-6}{\second}$ with
$f_\mathrm{MR}=1$, $1.25$, $1.41$, $2$, and $3$, and the working configuration
is selected from this sweep. At this target the energy criterion no longer
discriminates between the meshes. Every run stays below the
\SI{5}{\percent} guideline over the forming window in
Figure~\ref{fig:confirm_sweep_results}, with late energy ratios below
\SI{1}{\percent}. The selection therefore rests on the fracture strains and
run times in Table~\ref{tab:confirm_sweep_summary}.

The relative changes in Table~\ref{tab:confirm_sweep_summary} compare each
mesh with the next finer one, so each value measures the effect of a single
coarsening step. The minor strain is insensitive to the mesh, and mesh
convergence is therefore judged on $\varepsilon_1^\mathrm{f}$. The fine-side
settings $f_\mathrm{MR}=1$ to $1.41$ form a plateau with changes of at most a
few percent, while the coarser settings show large and non-monotonic changes,
which indicates that they do not resolve the strain localisation at the
fracture site. The run time falls monotonically with coarsening, and the
coarser runs were also allocated fewer cores, so the time differences
understate the true cost differences.

The setting $\Delta t_\mathrm{MS}=\SI{1e-6}{\second}$ with
$f_\mathrm{MR}=1.41$ is therefore adopted as the working configuration. It
offers the best compromise between accuracy and computational resources,
since it stays on the fine-side plateau at the shortest run time, while also
conforming to the quasi-static requirement of the energy criterion.

\subsection{Friction-Factor Sensitivity Check}
\label{sec:friction_sensitivity}

The contact check varies only the punch--blank Coulomb coefficient
$\mu_\mathrm{punch}$ from 0 to 0.25 for the \texttt{W120} specimen at
$t=\SI{1.5}{\milli\meter}$ and $\alpha=\SI{0}{\degree}$. The runs use
$\Delta t_\mathrm{MS}=\SI{1e-6}{\second}$, $f_\mathrm{MR}=1$, and a prescribed
punch travel of \SI{32}{\milli\meter}. This is a single-width sensitivity check,
not a calibrated numerical FLC. The late energy check remains below
\SI{5}{\percent} for every friction value
(Table~\ref{tab:friction_sensitivity_summary}), so the discussion focuses on
the strain path, force response, and fracture location.

Only the frictionless case gives a dome-centred fracture. All nonzero punch
friction cases fail outside the dome evaluation zone, so the extracted
fracture strains describe different failure sites, not one dome-formability
trend. The apparent early failure at $\mu_\mathrm{punch}=0.05$ in
Table~\ref{tab:friction_sensitivity_summary} is an evaluation artifact rather
than a structural failure. Its punch force rises monotonically to
\SI{17.5}{\kilo\newton} over the full \SI{32}{\milli\meter} travel without
any force drop, and the extracted point corresponds to an early localised
element deletion outside the dome at a low damage level. The force curves for
the remaining friction values from 0.10 to 0.25 stay close until late
forming, so the main effect of punch friction in this sweep is the shift of
the failure site and of the failure instant. The largest effect on the
extracted strains appears in $\varepsilon_2^\mathrm{f}$, which decreases from
0.184 in the frictionless case to 0.047 at $\mu_\mathrm{punch}=0.25$.
Increasing punch friction suppresses the biaxial stretching at the dome apex
and moves the critical strain path toward plane strain.

The frictionless baseline, which matches the PTFE and lanolin lubrication of
the experiments, is the only case of this sweep that yields a valid
dome-centred limit point. A defensible friction choice should still be
calibrated against the experimental force-displacement response and rechecked
at the working setting $f_\mathrm{MR}=1.41$.

\subsection{Current Numerical Output Status}
\label{sec:numerical_output_status}

At the present report stage, the numerical runs verify the pipeline and fix a
defensible reference setting for the \texttt{W100} case. They do not yet
construct a numerical FLC. The remaining work is to run the working settings
across the relevant specimen widths and to validate the force response and
failure locations against the experiments.
