\chapter{Results}
\label{chap:results}

\section{Task I: Experimental Results}
\label{sec:results_task1}
This section reports the main experimental observations obtained from the
forming test campaign. The focus is placed on the influence of material
orientation, punch diameter, and sheet thickness on the experimentally
identified forming limits.


\subsection{Influence of Material Orientation}
\label{sec:orientation_influence}

A dedicated subset of the campaign was used to isolate the influence of
material orientation on the experimentally determined forming limits. For
every sheet thickness (A--D), the standard Nakazima test was performed with
specimens oriented in the rolling direction (RD, configuration
\texttt{E0\_RM00\_000}) and in the transverse direction (TD, configuration
\texttt{E0\_RM00\_090}). The suffix denotes the orientation
angle $\alpha$ relative to RD, with \SI{0}{\degree} for RD and
\SI{90}{\degree} for TD. The punch diameter, specimen widths, and test-bench
settings were kept identical between the two orientations, so that
differences in the resulting forming limits can be interpreted primarily as
orientation effects.

For each orientation and thickness, two complete campaigns of seven specimen
widths were performed, and the forming-limit points reported here are the mean
over the two campaigns, which reduces the influence of test specific scatter.
The limit points were determined from
the DIC strain fields using the ISO~12004-2 intersection-line method
described in Section~\ref{sec:din_method}. Figure~\ref{fig:rd_vs_td} overlays the resulting RD and TD forming
limit curves for the four thicknesses.

Across all four thicknesses, the RD and TD forming limit curves are nearly
coincident, following the same trajectory from the uniaxial strain-state through the
plane-strain minimum and into the biaxial strain-state. The differences in
Table~\ref{tab:rd_td_summary} remain within $\pm0.019$ in $\varepsilon_1$ and
$\pm0.017$ in $\varepsilon_2$. The largest differences
occur at the equi-biaxial \texttt{W200} point of the thickest sheet~D, where the transverse case has only one usable repeat. Without a second repetition this point retains the full test dispersion that averaging removes everywhere else, so part of its larger difference is attributable to test to test variation rather than to orientation. 

These results indicate that, for the AA5754 sheet material and thickness range
investigated here, in-plane material orientation produces no systematic shift in
the forming limits beyond the repeat scatter of the campaign. This justifies
restricting the remaining punch-diameter tests to the rolling direction and
supports using one orientation-independent experimental FLC reference for this
dataset.

\subsection{Punch Diameter, Sheet Thickness, and the Evaluation Window}
\label{sec:punch_thickness}

The second experimental study varies the punch diameter
($\varnothing$\SI{40}{\milli\meter}, $\varnothing$\SI{60}{\milli\meter},
$\varnothing$\SI{80}{\milli\meter}, and
$\varnothing$\SI{100}{\milli\meter}) across the four sheet thicknesses (A--D)
to expose the coupling between punch geometry and sheet thickness. Physically,
a smaller punch superimposes more bending on the membrane stretch, since
wrapping a sheet of thickness $t$ around a punch of radius $R$ adds a bending
strain of about $t/2R$ between the outer surface and the mid plane. This
through thickness strain gradient, together with the contact pressure of the
punch, stabilises the deformation against localised necking and raises the
limit strains measured on the outer
surface~\cite{Charpentier1975, Min2016}. Both effects scale with the ratio
$t/R$. Two trends are therefore expected: the FLC should rise
as the punch diameter decreases, and the spread between punch diameters
should widen with increasing thickness, since $t/R$ grows fastest for the
smallest punch on the thickest sheet.

Figure~\ref{fig:flc_norm} shows the forming limit curves obtained with the
standard ISO~12004-2 evaluation, using the window width of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:din_window} with its calibration constant of $10$. The
\SI{100}{\milli\meter}, \SI{80}{\milli\meter}, and \SI{60}{\milli\meter}
punches form a closely spaced group, but the diameter ordering is not preserved
throughout the ISO result. The \SI{40}{\milli\meter} punch does not show a
consistent upward shift relative to the larger punches. Instead, parts of its
FLC are lower than the larger-punch group, and the sequence of the curves
changes with sheet thickness and strain state. This behaviour is not
compatible with a simple curvature driven size effect, which would be expected
to vary continuously with punch diameter. It points to an interaction between
the fixed ISO fitting window and the neck geometry of the nonstandard punches.
The standard window is calibrated for the
$\varnothing$\SI{100}{\milli\meter} Nakazima geometry, whereas the
\SI{40}{\milli\meter} punch produces a much narrower neck, so a window sized
for the standard punch no longer matches the strain field to which it is
applied.

The intersection-line method is governed by two length scales. The first is the
spacing between neighbouring intersection lines, fixed here at
\SI{2}{\milli\meter}. The second is the neck-window width used for the ISO
parabola fit, introduced through the constant $10$ in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:din_window}. These choices do not represent the same physical
scale. The line spacing controls how densely the strain field is sampled near
the crack path, whereas the fitting window controls how much of the neck
profile is used to identify the limit strain. The latter is expected to depend
on punch size because the strained dome and neck profile become narrower when
the punch diameter $D$ decreases. A constant calibrated on the
$\varnothing$\SI{100}{\milli\meter} ISO punch is therefore not necessarily
transferable to the \SI{80}{\milli\meter}, \SI{60}{\milli\meter}, and
\SI{40}{\milli\meter} punches.

As a diagnostic, the window sensitivity is first checked with deliberately
smaller fixed constants. Figure~\ref{fig:flc_wvar} compares constants 10, 9, 8,
and 7. This is not a recalibration, but a way to test whether a reduced window
moves the curves towards a more consistent punch-diameter ordering. The
observed shift motivates a simple geometry-scaled candidate, replacing the
fixed constant by $D/10$. The window width then becomes
$\omega = (D/10)\,[1 + \bar{\varepsilon}_2/\bar{\varepsilon}_1]$, which
preserves the standard value for the $\varnothing$\SI{100}{\milli\meter} punch
and reduces the window for the smaller punches. Figure~\ref{fig:flc_pscaled}
shows the corresponding re-evaluation.

These observations indicate that the ISO~12004-2 evaluation, calibrated for a
single standardised punch, cannot be transferred unchanged to a campaign that
varies the punch diameter. The window width and, by the
same argument, the intersection-line spacing should be made tunable and
geometry-aware, scaling with the punch diameter and the sheet thickness,
respectively. The $D/10$ rule is a first, deliberately simple and defensible candidate. 

\section{Task II: Min--Stoughton Curvature Criterion}
\label{sec:results_task2}

The standalone Min--Stoughton Nakazima workflow described in
Section~\ref{sec:minstoughton_workflow_assessment} was applied to the complete
rolling-direction campaign of all four sheet thicknesses, A
($t=\SI{1.2}{\milli\meter}$), B ($t=\SI{1.5}{\milli\meter}$), C
($t=\SI{2.0}{\milli\meter}$), and D ($t=\SI{3.0}{\milli\meter}$), with the
\SI{100}{\milli\meter} punch. Each thickness provides the seven Nakazima widths
from \texttt{W020} to \texttt{W200} in two repetitions, so the data set contains
\num{56} DIC samples. The runs were executed
from the \software{VIC-3D} exports, with the specimen metadata resolved as described in Table~\ref{tab:ms_constants}.

\subsection{Parameter selection on the reference specimens}
\label{sec:ms_reference}

The sensitivity to the matrix length $W_Y$ was studied first on the two
reference specimens, the narrow \texttt{W020} sample and the wide \texttt{W200}
sample. Tables~\ref{tab:minstoughton_w020_wy_sweep}
and~\ref{tab:minstoughton_w200_wy_sweep} report this sweep. For both samples the
setting $W_Y=\SI{15}{\milli\meter}$ gives the closest agreement with the
ISO~12004-2 average necking result. It should be noted that this choice is
agreement driven rather than stability driven, since the longer matrices form
a plateau with an identical onset for \texttt{W020} while
$W_Y=\SI{15}{\milli\meter}$ deviates from it. The \texttt{W020} coverage also
decreases as $W_Y$ increases because the requested matrix extends beyond the
valid DIC field for the narrow specimen. The value
$W_Y=\SI{15}{\milli\meter}$ is nevertheless used for the campaign analysis
that follows, and the sweep of Section~\ref{sec:ms_sweep_thickness} revisits
the parameter choice per thickness.

As Figure~\ref{fig:flc_ms_comparison} shows, the curvature criterion detects an
onset in 13 of 14 samples for both class~A and class~B, 12 of 14 for class~C,
and all 14 for class~D, the non-detections being isolated
\texttt{no\_sustained\_exceedance} cases at a single width. Per-width
repeatability is good through the body of the curve, with a standard deviation
below \num{0.014} from \texttt{W050} to \texttt{W120}, but it degrades at the
extreme geometries, where the narrow \texttt{W020} and the biaxial \texttt{W200}
samples occasionally trigger early or fail the persistence requirement. For the
thinner sheets the curvature limit stays close to the established methods: the
mean absolute major-strain difference to the ISO~12004-2 average is about
\num{0.014} for class~B, comparable to the \num{0.02} found for class~A. The agreement
degrades for the thicker sheets, rising to about \num{0.033} for class~C and
\num{0.046} for class~D, and class~D shows a consistent negative bias, with the
curvature major strain falling below both reference methods at nearly every
width, for example $\varepsilon_1=\num{0.285}$ against the ISO~12004-2
\num{0.358} at \texttt{W020}. This trend indicates that the parameters
calibrated on the thin material do not transfer unchanged to thicker sheets,
where the stronger through-thickness curvature alters the dimple signature so
that the fixed SAC and threshold trigger the onset earlier. A
thickness-dependent parameter choice is therefore required before the curvature
method can be applied for production forming-limit extraction across the full
thickness range.

\subsection{Parameter Sweep Across Thicknesses}
\label{sec:ms_sweep_thickness}

To test whether the thicker-sheet underestimation can be removed by retuning
rather than being intrinsic, a parameter sweep was run over all four thicknesses
(\num{56} samples), covering $W_Y\in\{15,20,25\}\,\si{\milli\meter}$,
$M\in\{0.70,0.75,0.80\}$, $n\in\{6,8,10\}$,
$\mathrm{SAC}\in\{\num{5e-4},\num{1e-3}\}\,\si{\per\milli\meter}$, and
$\alpha\in\{0.10,0.20\}$, that is \num{108} parameter sets per sample. Each set
was scored by its detection rate and, as a single agreement measure, by the mean
Euclidean distance in the forming-limit diagram,
$d_{\mathrm{ISO}}=\big\langle\sqrt{(\Delta\varepsilon_1)^2+(\Delta\varepsilon_2)^2}\big\rangle$,
of the curvature point to the ISO~12004-2 average over the detected widths, so
that both the major and the minor limit strain enter the ranking.
Table~\ref{tab:ms_sweep_thickness} compares the standard set with the best
agreeing set (minimum $d_{\mathrm{ISO}}$, detecting at least 13 of the 14
samples) for each thickness. The complete sweep is tabulated in
Appendix~\ref{app:ms_sweep}.

Requiring detection of all fourteen samples is not always the best choice. For
the thinner sheets the fourteenth sample is the stubborn second repetition of

benefits most, its distance dropping from \num{0.037} to \num{0.019} once the
threshold multiplier is raised to $\alpha=0.20$ with a longer matrix
($W_Y=\SI{25}{\milli\meter}$), and class~D improves from \num{0.051} to
\num{0.036}. A larger threshold delays the detected onset, which raises the
extracted strain and counteracts the underestimation seen in
Section~\ref{sec:ms_campaign}. The parameters were selected by minimising the
distance to the ISO~12004-2 average rather than validated against an
independent reference, so the values are indicative. A held out validation,
for example tuning on the first repetition and evaluating on the second, is
the natural next step before these sets are adopted. A thickness-dependent
parameter choice, with a larger curvature threshold for thicker sheets,
remains the recommended path before the curvature method is used for
production forming-limit extraction.

\section{Task III: Numerical Framework Verification Status}
\label{sec:numerical_results}

This section reports the verification status of the numerical framework rather
than a completed numerical FLC campaign. Because the material constants are
the AA5182 surrogate of Chapter~\ref{chap:numerical_model}, the runs are
interpreted as framework verification cases rather than quantitative
predictions for the AA5754 campaign. The framework, its outputs, and the full
inventory of adjustable settings are described in
Chapter~\ref{chap:numerical_model} and
Appendix~\ref{app:configuration_parameters}, and each numerical result below
is reported together with the configuration that generated it.

\subsection{Sensitivity Study}
\label{sec:sensitivity_study}

The sensitivity study determines suitable numerical parameters for the
production simulations. All runs use the fixed punch velocity and smooth step
profile of Chapter~\ref{chap:numerical_model}, frictionless punch contact,
and the Nakazima \texttt{W100} specimen with the \SI{100}{\milli\meter} punch
as the single reference case. The two numerical controls varied are the
mass-scaling target time increment and the mesh-refinement level. An
$\mathrm{ALLKE}/\mathrm{ALLIE}$ ratio below \SI{5}{\percent} is used as the
quasi-static acceptance criterion. For the mass-scaling screening, the
post-start maximum is used as a conservative metric. For the mesh-refinement
confirmation sweep, this is complemented by the maximum in the final
\SI{0.5}{\second} before the element-status fracture point, because the early
acceleration peak is not representative of the forming-limit extraction. This
split does not soften the mass-scaling selection, since for aggressive mass
scaling the exceedance persists into the extraction window, with a late-window
maximum of about \SI{11}{\percent} at
$\Delta t_\mathrm{MS}=\SI{1e-5}{\second}$, whereas for the fine meshes at the
accepted target the exceedance is confined to the early acceleration phase.

\subsubsection{Mass Scaling Analysis}
\label{sec:mass_scaling_analysis}

The mass-scaling sweep varies the target time increment while keeping the mesh
refinement factor fixed at $f_\mathrm{MR}=2$. The evaluated target increments are
$1\times10^{-6}$, $5\times10^{-6}$, $1\times10^{-5}$, $5\times10^{-5}$, and
$1\times10^{-4}$\,\si{\second}.

Figure~\ref{fig:ms_sweep_results} shows that this criterion is restrictive for
the present \texttt{W100} reference case. The full-range energy plot gives the overall
magnitude of the inertial response, while the \SIrange{0}{10}{\percent} zoom
resolves the neighbourhood of the \SI{5}{\percent} criterion. After the initial
contact transient is omitted, only
$\Delta t_\mathrm{MS}=\SI{1e-6}{\second}$ remains below the
\SI{5}{\percent} $\mathrm{ALLKE}/\mathrm{ALLIE}$ limit. The larger target time
increments produce visibly similar strain paths over much of the process,
but their energy ratios are too high for a quasi-static production setting.
This quasi-static requirement is also the computationally expensive choice:
Table~\ref{tab:mass_scaling_walltime} shows that the only target that passes
the energy criterion, $\Delta t_\mathrm{MS}=\SI{1e-6}{\second}$, costs about
\SI{95}{\times} the solver wall-clock time of the fastest but non-quasi-static
target, $\Delta t_\mathrm{MS}=\SI{1e-4}{\second}$.

\subsubsection{Mesh Refinement Analysis}
\label{sec:mesh_refinement_analysis}

The mesh-refinement sweep varies the global mesh-refinement factor while keeping
the mass-scaling target fixed at
$\Delta t_\mathrm{MS}=\SI{1e-5}{\second}$. The
evaluated refinement factors are $1$, $2$, and $3$. The meshes are generated
automatically by sweeping the global refinement factor of
Section~\ref{sec:mesh_generation}, which scales all section sizes together while
leaving the sectioning unchanged (Figure~\ref{fig:mesh_mr_sweep}). Since strain
localisation is mesh sensitive, the production mesh should be the coarsest
discretisation for which the extracted forming-limit point has converged.

The strain paths in Figure~\ref{fig:mr_sweep_results} are close over most of
the deformation history, indicating that the \texttt{W100} response is not
strongly affected by the tested mesh-refinement factors before the final
localisation stage. The accompanying energy overview and
\SIrange{0}{10}{\percent} zoom also show that the fixed
$\Delta t_\mathrm{MS}=\SI{1e-5}{\second}$ target is not yet an acceptable
quasi-static setting for this mesh study. Increasing $f_\mathrm{MR}$ reduces the
energy ratio, but the curves remain above the \SI{5}{\percent} limit over part
of the simulation. A production mesh choice should therefore be confirmed with a
mesh-refinement sweep repeated at the accepted mass-scaling target.
Table~\ref{tab:mesh_refinement_walltime} shows the solver wall-clock time of
this diagnostic sweep; the coarser meshes are cheaper, as expected, but the
comparison is only indicative here since none of the three settings meet the
energy criterion at this mass-scaling target.

\subsubsection{Selection of Numerical Parameters}
\label{sec:selection_numerical_parameters}

The final production settings combine the mass-scaling and mesh-refinement
sweeps. The target is the least expensive configuration that satisfies both
the quasi-static energy criterion and the strain-convergence criterion.

For the completed \texttt{W100} reference runs, the mass-scaling sweep identifies
$\Delta t_\mathrm{MS}=\SI{1e-6}{\second}$ as the only tested target satisfying
the \SI{5}{\percent} energy criterion. The mesh-refinement sweep at
$\Delta t_\mathrm{MS}=\SI{1e-5}{\second}$ is useful as a discretisation
diagnostic, but is not used alone to select production settings because the
quasi-static criterion is violated there. The mesh-refinement comparison was
therefore repeated at the accepted mass-scaling target
$\Delta t_\mathrm{MS}=\SI{1e-6}{\second}$. The completed reference runs cover
$f_\mathrm{MR}=1$, $1.25$, $1.41$, $2$, and $3$, so that the selected
production mesh is judged under the same quasi-static setting used for the
final simulations.

Figure~\ref{fig:confirm_sweep_results} and
Table~\ref{tab:confirm_sweep_summary} separate the early post-start peak from
the energy ratio in the forming-limit window. The post-start maximum remains
high for $f_\mathrm{MR}=1$, $1.25$, and $1.41$, reaching
\SI{23.9}{\percent}, \SI{24.5}{\percent}, and \SI{41.4}{\percent}. These peaks
occur during the early acceleration part of the simulation. In the final
\SI{0.5}{\second} before fracture, all completed confirmation runs are below
the \SI{5}{\percent} criterion, with late-window maxima between
\SI{0.18}{\percent} and \SI{0.75}{\percent}
(Table~\ref{tab:confirm_sweep_summary}).

The fine-side checks $f_\mathrm{MR}=1$, $1.25$, and $1.41$ give close
element-status fracture strains, with $\varepsilon_1^\mathrm{f}$ between
\num{0.201} and \num{0.207}, and $\varepsilon_2^\mathrm{f}$ between
\num{0.096} and \num{0.097}, which supports mesh convergence on the fine
side (Figure~\ref{fig:ms_mr_flp}). The coarser settings $f_\mathrm{MR}=2$ and
$3$ keep the minor strain stable, but their major fracture strains lie above
this plateau, at \num{0.244} and \num{0.219}, so neither can be considered
converged. The production settings are therefore fixed at
$\Delta t_\mathrm{MS}=\SI{1e-6}{\second}$ and $f_\mathrm{MR}=1.41$ for the
remainder of this report, since this mesh lies on the converged fine-side
plateau and is at the same time the least expensive run of the confirmation
sweep (Table~\ref{tab:confirm_sweep_summary}). Because the refinement factor
scales the seed spacing, $f_\mathrm{MR}=1.41\approx\sqrt{2}$ corresponds to
about half the in plane element count of the finest tested mesh, obtained at
roughly a quarter of its computational cost. Broader mesh-independence
checks across more geometries are left as future work
(Chapter~\ref{chap:future_work}).

\subsection{Friction-Factor Sensitivity Check}
\label{sec:friction_sensitivity}

In addition to the mass-scaling and mesh-refinement sweeps, a local contact
sensitivity check was performed for one Nakazima
\SI{100}{\milli\meter}-punch geometry. The runs use the \texttt{W120} specimen at
$t=\SI{1.5}{\milli\meter}$, $\alpha=\SI{0}{\degree}$,
$\Delta t_\mathrm{MS}=\SI{1e-6}{\second}$, $f_\mathrm{MR}=1$, and a prescribed
punch travel of \SI{32}{\milli\meter}. Only the punch--blank Coulomb
coefficient $\mu_\mathrm{punch}$ was varied, from 0 to 0.25. The die and
blank-holder contact coefficient remained at the default value of 0.35. This
is therefore a single-width sensitivity check rather than a calibrated
numerical FLC. The runs use the finest mesh of the confirmation sweep of
Section~\ref{sec:selection_numerical_parameters} rather than the production
setting $f_\mathrm{MR}=1.41$, so the mid-process energy ratios are elevated. In
the final \SI{0.5}{\second} before each extracted fracture point the ratio
stays below the \SI{5}{\percent} criterion for every run
(Table~\ref{tab:friction_sensitivity_summary}).

The strain paths in Figure~\ref{fig:friction_sensitivity_results} show that
the extracted fracture point depends strongly on the assumed punch--blank
friction. The automated fracture-location classification of the post
processing explains this dependence. Only the frictionless case produces a
valid dome-centred fracture, while every run with nonzero punch friction
fails outside the dome evaluation zone, at two distinct locations on the
specimen (Table~\ref{tab:friction_sensitivity_summary}). This is consistent
with the physical role of punch friction in the Nakazima test, which
restrains the apex and shifts the straining towards the surrounding material.
The extracted fracture strains of the nonzero-friction runs therefore
describe different failure sites rather than a single dome-formability trend,
and the early failure of the $\mu_\mathrm{punch}=0.05$ case at
$U_3=\SI{18.6}{\milli\meter}$ is an early failure at one of these off-dome
sites rather than an anomaly among comparable dome fractures. The punch-force
histories remain nearly coincident up to the late forming stage for
$\mu_\mathrm{punch}=0.10$--0.25, so the main effect of the friction level is
a shift of the failure site and failure instant rather than a change of the
global force response.

where the specimen fails before it controls how much the dome can be
stretched. For this \texttt{W120} geometry, any nonzero punch friction moves
the failure outside the dome evaluation zone, so a numerical FLC campaign
with realistic friction must expect off-dome fractures, which the automated
fracture-location classification already detects and flags. The frictionless
baseline, which matches the PTFE and lanolin lubrication of the experiments,
is the only case of this sweep that yields a valid dome-centred limit point.
A defensible friction choice should be calibrated against the experimental
force--displacement response and then rechecked at the production settings
$\Delta t_\mathrm{MS}=\SI{1e-6}{\second}$ and $f_\mathrm{MR}=1.41$.

\subsection{Current Numerical Output Status}
\label{sec:numerical_output_status}

At the present report stage, the numerical runs verify the pipeline rather
than construct final numerical forming-limit curves. With the mass-scaling
