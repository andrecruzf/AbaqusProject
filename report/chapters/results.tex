\section{Task I: Experimental Results}
\label{sec:results_task1}
This section reports the main experimental observations obtained from the
forming test campaign. The focus is placed on the influence of material
orientation, punch diameter, and sheet thickness on the experimentally
identified forming limits.


\subsection{Influence of Material Orientation}
\label{sec:orientation_influence}

A dedicated subset of the campaign was used to isolate the influence of material orientation on the experimentally determined forming limits. For every sheet thickness (A--D), the \SI{100}{\milli\meter} hemispherical punch was tested with the specimen long axis aligned both with the rolling direction (RD, configuration \texttt{E0\_RM00\_000}) and with the transverse direction (TD, configuration \texttt{E0\_RM00\_090}). The configuration label encodes the punch and orientation: the trailing \texttt{000} and \texttt{090} denote the in-plane angle $\alpha$ between the specimen long axis and the rolling direction, so that \texttt{E0\_RM00\_000} corresponds to RD ($\alpha = \SI{0}{\degree}$) and \texttt{E0\_RM00\_090} to TD ($\alpha = \SI{90}{\degree}$). All other parameters (punch diameter, specimen widths, and test bench settings) are held identical between the two orientations, so that any difference in the resulting forming limits can be attributed to anisotropy alone.

For each orientation and thickness, two complete campaigns of seven specimen widths were performed, and the forming-limit points reported here are the mean over the two campaigns. Averaging over two independent sets reduces the influence of anomalies that a single set may carry, such as local material defects, specimen handling or operator errors, or other test-specific outliers, so that the reported curve reflects the representative material response rather than the particular condition of one set. The limit points themselves were determined from the DIC strain fields using the section-line (DIN) method of DIN EN ISO 12004-2~\cite{ISO120042}, in which strain profiles are evaluated along section lines placed across the necking zone and the limit strains are recovered from an inverse best-fit of these profiles. The same evaluation pipeline used throughout this work was applied, enforcing both the robustness and accuracy selection criteria. Figure~\ref{fig:rd_vs_td} overlays the resulting RD and TD forming limit curves for the four thicknesses.

Across all four thicknesses the RD and TD forming limit curves are essentially coincident. The two orientations follow the same trajectory from the uniaxial branch through the plane-strain minimum and into the biaxial branch, and the residual differences between them remain within the scatter of the underlying campaigns. The separation between the RD and TD curves is consistently smaller than the spread observed between the two repeated campaigns of a single orientation, so it cannot be distinguished from experimental repeatability. No systematic trend with thickness is observed: the orientations do not diverge more strongly for the thinner sheets (A, B) than for the thicker ones (C, D).

These results indicate that, for the AA5754 sheet material and thickness range investigated here, in-plane material orientation has no measurable influence on the forming limits. This justifies restricting the remaining punch-diameter campaigns to the rolling direction and supports the use of an in-plane isotropic description of the forming limits in the numerical model developed in Chapter~\ref{chap:numerical_model}.

\subsection{Punch Diameter, Sheet Thickness, and the Evaluation Window}
\label{sec:punch_thickness}

The second experimental study varies the punch diameter ($\varnothing$40, 60, 80, and 100\,mm) across the four sheet thicknesses (A--D) to expose the coupling between punch geometry and sheet thickness. Physically, a smaller punch superimposes more bending on the membrane stretch and produces a steeper through-thickness strain gradient, which stabilises the deformation against localised necking. The magnitude of this effect scales with the ratio $t/R$ of sheet thickness to punch radius. Two trends are therefore expected: the forming limit curve should rise as the punch diameter decreases, and the spread between punch diameters should widen with increasing thickness, since $t/R$ grows fastest for the smallest punch on the thickest sheet.

Figure~\ref{fig:flc_norm} shows the forming limit curves obtained with the standard ISO~12004-2 evaluation, that is, the intersection-line method of Section~\ref{sec:din_method} using the window width of Eq.~\eqref{eq:din_window} with its calibration constant of $10$. For the 100, 80, and 60\,mm punches the expected ordering is recovered, but the 40\,mm punch does not follow it: its limit curve lies far above the others, by a margin that grows with thickness and that is too large to be attributed to the $t/R$ curvature effect alone. Because all curves in a given diagram originate from the same material and thickness, this incoherence points to the evaluation rather than to the material response. The standard window is calibrated for the $\varnothing$100\,mm Nakazima geometry, whereas the 40\,mm punch produces a much narrower neck, so a window sized for the standard punch no longer matches the strain field to which it is applied.

The intersection-line method exposes two fixed length-scale parameters: the spacing between intersection lines ($2$\,mm) and the constant factor of $10$ in the window width of Eq.~\eqref{eq:din_window}. These correspond to two physically distinct scales. The fitting window is governed by the extent of the strained dome, which scales with the punch diameter $D$, whereas the excluded near-crack zone is governed by the neck width, which scales with the sheet thickness $t$. The fixed constant captures neither. A minimal, norm-consistent generalisation replaces the constant with $D/10$, so that the window width becomes $\omega = (D/10)\,[1 + \bar{\varepsilon}_2/\bar{\varepsilon}_1]$ and reduces to the standard value for the $\varnothing$100\,mm punch ($100/10 = 10$). Figure~\ref{fig:flc_pscaled} shows the limit curves re-evaluated with this punch-scaled window. Scaling the window with the punch diameter markedly shifts the extracted 40\,mm limit, which confirms that its position under the standard evaluation is governed largely by the window choice rather than by the material.

These observations indicate that the ISO~12004-2 evaluation, calibrated for a single standardised punch, cannot be transferred unchanged to a campaign that varies the punch diameter: the extracted 40\,mm limit reflects the fixed window as much as the material response. The window width and, by the same argument, the intersection-line spacing should be made tunable and geometry-aware, scaling with the punch diameter and the sheet thickness respectively. The $D/10$ rule is a first, deliberately simple candidate. A defensible parameter choice additionally requires verifying that the extracted limit is insensitive to the window in the neighbourhood of the selected value (a convergence criterion), and cross-validating against the window-independent criteria of Sections~\ref{sec:vh_method} and~\ref{sec:minstoughton_method} (Volk \& Hora and Min--Stoughton). This validation is left to future work.

\section{Task II: FLC Identification Workflow}
\label{sec:results_task2}

The results chapter focuses on the implemented numerical workflow and its
interfaces to the experimental campaign and identification framework. For each
selected configuration, the pipeline creates the Abaqus model, submits the
solver job on Euler, runs ODB-based post-processing, and synchronises structured
result artefacts. The same interface is used for Nakazima, Marciniak, and
Punch-in-Punch simulations, so that specimen width, sheet thickness, punch
radius, punch travel, material orientation, mass scaling, and mesh refinement
can be varied without changing the post-processing schema.

Each completed simulation produces the same set of data products:
\texttt{punch\_fd.csv}, \texttt{energy\_data.csv}, \texttt{strain\_path.csv},
\texttt{forming\_limits.csv}, diagnostic figures, and movie files. This common
output structure is important for the broader project because the numerical
data can be compared directly with Task~I experiments and passed to the Task~II
FLC identification framework.

\subsection{Reported Simulation Configurations}

Since the numerical framework is highly parameterised, the results are reported
together with the configuration that generated them. The full inventory of

The present chapter only repeats the concrete values that are needed to
interpret a specific comparison. This separation keeps
Chapter~\ref{chap:numerical_model} focused on the modelling framework itself,

\section{Numerical Simulation Results}
\label{sec:numerical_results}

The numerical results presented in this section focus on the validation of the
simulation settings and on representative forming results obtained with the
automated framework. The general structure and capabilities of the framework
were introduced in Chapter~\ref{chap:numerical_model}; they are therefore not
repeated here.

\subsection{Quasi-Static Validation}
\label{sec:quasi_static_validation}

The simulations were carried out using Abaqus/Explicit and must therefore be
checked for quasi-static behaviour. For this purpose, the ratio between kinetic
energy and internal energy, $\mathrm{ALLKE}/\mathrm{ALLIE}$, was monitored
throughout the forming step. A ratio below 5\% was used as the acceptance
criterion.

\subsection{Element-Size Control Across Configurations}
\label{sec:mesh_control}

A central requirement of this work is that the element size is controlled in a
reproducible, geometry-independent way, so that simulations of different
specimen widths and punch configurations can be compared without the mesh acting
as a hidden variable. This is handled by the modular meshing tool, which seeds
the specimen from a single radius-based zone table rather than from a manually
built mesh per model. Because the resulting discretisation is produced entirely
at the meshing stage and does not depend on the solver, the element-size control
can be generated and verified at negligible cost, independently of the more
expensive forming simulations.

The in-plane discretisation is defined by a fixed table of concentric seeding
zones (Table~\ref{tab:mesh_zones}). Each edge is assigned the target size of the
first zone whose outer radius exceeds the edge radius, so the element size is a
function of radial position alone. Two properties follow directly. First, at any
given radius the target element size is identical across all specimen widths and
punch diameters, because the same zone table is applied to every geometry; the
specimen width only determines how many of the outer zones are present, since
outer zones are silently skipped on narrow specimens. Second, the apex zone
($r < 5$\,mm) can be locked to a fixed size during refinement studies, so the
critical fracture region retains a constant resolution while the surrounding mesh
is varied. Through the thickness, the sheet is seeded with a fixed number of
element layers ($N_\mathrm{thk} = 10$), so the through-thickness resolution is
likewise constant across configurations. The partitioned topology and the radial
and circumferential seeding controls that expose these zones are shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:bm_mesh_zones}.

The overall mesh density is driven by a single global mesh refinement factor
$f_\mathrm{MR}$, which scales the in-plane target sizes uniformly on top of the
fixed partition topology ($f_\mathrm{MR}=1$ baseline, $f_\mathrm{MR}=0.5$ finer,
$f_\mathrm{MR}=2$ coarser). Because refinement is a single configuration value
rather than a manual remeshing step, the mesh-size study reported in
Section~\ref{sec:mesh_refinement_analysis} is generated automatically by sweeping
$f_\mathrm{MR}$, which realises the user-defined automated mesh-size modification
targeted by the framework. Figure~\ref{fig:mesh_mr_sweep} illustrates this on the
$W100$ Nakazima specimen for $f_\mathrm{MR}=1$, $2$, and $4$: the partition
topology and the locked apex zone are preserved across the three meshes, while
the element size in the surrounding zones scales with the refinement factor.

Beyond a uniform refinement, the seeding controls also allow the element size to
grow at different rates from the fine apex region towards the coarse flange. The
target size assigned to each radial section is set independently, so the ratio
between adjacent sections, that is, the rate at which the mesh coarsens outwards,
can be tuned while keeping the apex resolution fixed. Figure~\ref{fig:mesh_growth}
shows the $W100$ Nakazima specimen meshed with the same $0.2$\,mm apex size but
section-to-section growth ratios of $1.5$, $2.0$, and $3.0$. A gentle ratio keeps
a fine mesh across the whole dome at a higher element count, whereas an
aggressive ratio concentrates the elements near the apex and coarsens rapidly
towards the flange, reducing the model size at the cost of dome resolution.

The global refinement factor and the section-to-section growth rate shown above
are only two particular combinations drawn from a much larger control space. The
meshing model is deliberately modular: rather than exposing a single element
size, it exposes the full partition layout and an independent target size for
every section, in both the radial and circumferential directions, together with
the number of through-thickness layers. The two figures above are therefore
representative examples, and many further meshes can be produced by combining
these settings differently, for example refining one region while coarsening
another, or shifting the partition boundaries themselves.

These controls are organised around the partitioned mesh-zone layout shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:mesh_gui}, which the pipeline exposes both through its web
interface and directly in the configuration file, so that the same mesh can be
defined interactively or scripted in code. The diagram maps the two specimen
families (W20--W120 and W200) and assigns each coloured region a named section:
S1 the finest centre zone, S2 the transition, S3 the outer transition, the S3\_1
band specific to W20/W50, and S4 the outer coarse band. Two groups of variables
act on this layout. The \emph{partition geometry} (the inner split $x$, inner arc
radius $P_\mathrm{inner\_r}$, circle radius $P_\mathrm{circle\_r}$, and the
$\mathrm{XZ}$-plane position, together with the W200 radii $P_\mathrm{section\,1\,y}$,
$P_\mathrm{section\,2\,r}$, and $P_\mathrm{section\,3\,r}$) sets the boundaries
between sections, that is, it decides \emph{where} the mesh density changes. The
\emph{target element size} assigned to each section (S1--S4, independently in the
radial and circumferential directions) sets the element size \emph{within} that
section. A global mesh factor and a through-thickness seed count complete the
set. The refinement sweep of Figure~\ref{fig:mesh_mr_sweep} corresponds to
scaling all section sizes by the global factor, while the growth-rate study of
Figure~\ref{fig:mesh_growth} corresponds to changing the ratio between adjacent
section sizes; both are thus special cases of this same underlying control set.

\subsection{Sensitivity Study}
\label{sec:sensitivity_study}

A sensitivity study was performed to determine suitable numerical parameters
for the production simulations. The two investigated parameters were the mass
scaling target time increment and the mesh refinement level.

\subsubsection{Mass Scaling Analysis}
\label{sec:mass_scaling_analysis}

The influence of mass scaling was evaluated by comparing the
$\mathrm{ALLKE}/\mathrm{ALLIE}$ ratio for different target time increments.
Increasing the target time increment reduces the computational cost, but also
increases the risk of inertial effects. The selected value was therefore chosen
as the largest time increment that still satisfied the quasi-static energy
criterion.

\subsubsection{Mesh Refinement Analysis}
\label{sec:mesh_refinement_analysis}

The influence of mesh refinement was assessed by comparing the extracted
failure strain for different element sizes. Since strain localization is
mesh-sensitive, the selected mesh had to provide a stable forming-limit point
while keeping the computational cost acceptable.

\subsubsection{Selection of Numerical Parameters}
\label{sec:selection_numerical_parameters}

The final production settings were selected by combining the mass scaling and
mesh refinement results. The chosen parameter set corresponds to the cheapest
configuration that satisfies both the quasi-static energy criterion and the
strain convergence criterion.

\subsection{Strain Localization and Failure Behaviour}
\label{sec:strain_localisation_failure}

Representative contour plots of equivalent plastic strain or major strain are
used to verify the localization pattern before failure. These plots provide a
qualitative check that the extracted forming-limit points correspond to a
physically meaningful localization mode.
