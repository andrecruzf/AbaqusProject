\chapter{Results}
\label{chap:results}

\section{Task I: Experimental Results}
\label{sec:results_task1}
This section reports the main experimental observations obtained from the
forming test campaign. The focus is placed on the influence of material
orientation, punch diameter, and sheet thickness on the experimentally
identified forming limits.


\subsection{Influence of Material Orientation}
\label{sec:orientation_influence}

A dedicated subset of the campaign was used to isolate the influence of
material orientation on the experimentally determined forming limits. For every
sheet thickness (A--D), the \SI{100}{\milli\meter} hemispherical punch was
tested with the specimen long axis aligned with the rolling direction (RD,
configuration \texttt{E0\_RM00\_000}) and with the transverse direction (TD,
configuration \texttt{E0\_RM00\_090}). The relevant suffixes \texttt{000} and
\texttt{090} denote the in-plane angle $\alpha$ between the specimen long axis
and RD, so that \texttt{E0\_RM00\_000} corresponds to $\alpha=\SI{0}{\degree}$
and \texttt{E0\_RM00\_090} to $\alpha=\SI{90}{\degree}$. The punch diameter,
specimen widths, and test-bench settings were kept identical between the two
orientations, so that differences in the resulting forming limits can be
interpreted primarily as orientation effects.

For each orientation and thickness, two complete campaigns of seven specimen
widths were performed, and the forming-limit points reported here are the mean
over the two campaigns. Averaging over two independent sets reduces the
influence of test-specific scatter, so that the reported curve is less dependent
on the condition of one individual set. The limit points were determined from
the DIC strain fields using the ISO~12004-2 intersection-line method
described in Section~\ref{sec:din_method}. The same evaluation pipeline was
applied throughout this study, including the robustness and accuracy selection
criteria. Figure~\ref{fig:rd_vs_td} overlays the resulting RD and TD forming
limit curves for the four thicknesses.

Across all four thicknesses, the RD and TD forming limit curves are nearly
coincident, following the same trajectory from the uniaxial branch through the
plane-strain minimum and into the biaxial branch. Read per geometry, every
$d_{\mathrm{RD-TD}}$ in Table~\ref{tab:rd_td_summary} stays small, between
\num{0.0011} and \num{0.0252} strain, and remains of the same order as the
repeat scatter of the corresponding geometry in
Table~\ref{tab:rd_td_repeat_values}. No single width separates beyond its own
scatter: the largest values occur at the equi-biaxial \texttt{W200} point, most
notably for the thickest sheet~D, where the transverse \texttt{W200} case has
only one usable repeat. The signed differences also change sign between widths,
so the per-geometry offsets do not form a consistent orientation-dependent
shift, and no systematic trend with thickness is observed.

These results indicate that, for the AA5754 sheet material and thickness range
investigated here, in-plane material orientation has no measurable influence on
the forming limits within the repeatability of the campaign. This justifies
restricting the remaining punch-diameter tests to the rolling direction and
supports using one orientation-independent experimental FLC reference for this
dataset.

\subsection{Punch Diameter, Sheet Thickness, and the Evaluation Window}
\label{sec:punch_thickness}

The second experimental study varies the punch diameter
($\varnothing$\SI{40}{\milli\meter}, $\varnothing$\SI{60}{\milli\meter},
$\varnothing$\SI{80}{\milli\meter}, and
$\varnothing$\SI{100}{\milli\meter}) across the four sheet thicknesses (A--D)
to expose the coupling between punch geometry and sheet thickness. Physically,
a smaller punch superimposes more bending on the membrane stretch and produces
a steeper through-thickness strain gradient, which stabilises the deformation
against localised necking. The magnitude of this effect scales with the ratio
$t/R$ of sheet thickness to punch radius. Two trends are therefore expected:
the forming limit curve should rise as the punch diameter decreases, and the
spread between punch diameters should widen with increasing thickness, since
$t/R$ grows fastest for the smallest punch on the thickest sheet.

Figure~\ref{fig:flc_norm} shows the forming limit curves obtained with the
standard ISO~12004-2 evaluation, that is, the intersection-line method of
Section~\ref{sec:din_method} using the window width of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:din_window} with its calibration constant of $10$. The
\SI{100}{\milli\meter}, \SI{80}{\milli\meter}, and \SI{60}{\milli\meter}
punches form a smooth, monotonic progression: the limit curve rises gradually
as the punch diameter decreases, in line with the continuous $t/R$ curvature
trend. The \SI{40}{\milli\meter} punch breaks this continuity. Rather than
extending the trend by one further step, its limit curve jumps far above the
other three by a margin that grows with thickness. Because a genuine
curvature-driven size effect would vary continuously with punch diameter, an
isolated discontinuity at the smallest punch alone, on curves that otherwise
share the same material and thickness, points to the evaluation rather than to
the material response. The standard window is calibrated for the
$\varnothing$\SI{100}{\milli\meter} Nakazima geometry, whereas the
\SI{40}{\milli\meter} punch produces a much narrower neck, so a window sized
for the standard punch no longer matches the strain field to which it is
applied.

Table~\ref{tab:flc0_punch} quantifies this break through the plane-strain limit
$\mathrm{FLC}_0$, the major strain at $\varepsilon_2=0$. The limit rises
smoothly across the \SIlist{100;80;60}{\milli\meter} punches; extrapolating that
trend to \SI{40}{\milli\meter} underpredicts the measured limit by
$\Delta_{40}=+0.007$ for the thinnest sheet~A, growing to $+0.055$ for the
thickest sheet~D. This excess over the larger-punch trend, and its growth with
thickness, is consistent with the fixed standard window increasingly mis-fitting
the sharper neck of the small punch, rather than with a continuation of the
$t/R$ size effect.

The intersection-line method contains two fixed length-scale parameters: the
spacing between intersection lines, \SI{2}{\milli\meter}, and the constant
factor of $10$ in the window width of Eq.~\eqref{eq:din_window}. These
correspond to two physically distinct scales. The fitting window is governed by
the extent of the strained dome, which scales with the punch diameter $D$,
whereas the excluded near-crack zone is governed by the neck width, which
scales with the sheet thickness $t$. The fixed constant captures neither.
Alternative fixed window constants, including the 10, 9, 8, and 7 variants
inspected in the preliminary window study (Figure~\ref{fig:flc_wvar}), shift
the extracted curves but do not introduce a geometric length-scale relation.
A minimal, standard-consistent generalisation therefore replaces the constant
with $D/10$, so that the window width becomes
$\omega = (D/10)\,[1 + \bar{\varepsilon}_2/\bar{\varepsilon}_1]$ and reduces to
the standard value for the $\varnothing$\SI{100}{\milli\meter} punch
($100/10=10$). Figure~\ref{fig:flc_pscaled} shows the limit curves
re-evaluated with this punch-scaled window. Scaling the window with the punch
diameter markedly shifts the extracted \SI{40}{\milli\meter} limit, which
confirms that its position under the standard evaluation is governed largely by
the window choice rather than by the material.

These observations indicate that the ISO~12004-2 evaluation, calibrated for a
single standardised punch, cannot be transferred unchanged to a campaign that
varies the punch diameter: the extracted \SI{40}{\milli\meter} limit reflects
the fixed window as much as the material response. The window width and, by the
same argument, the intersection-line spacing should be made tunable and
geometry-aware, scaling with the punch diameter and the sheet thickness,
respectively. The $D/10$ rule is a first, deliberately simple candidate. A
defensible parameter choice additionally requires verifying that the extracted
limit is insensitive to the window in the neighbourhood of the selected value
and cross-validating against the criteria of Sections~\ref{sec:vh_method}
and~\ref{sec:minstoughton_method} (Volk--Hora and Min--Stoughton). This
validation is left to future work.

\section{Task II: Min--Stoughton Curvature Criterion}
\label{sec:results_task2}

The standalone Min--Stoughton Nakazima workflow described in
Section~\ref{sec:minstoughton_workflow_assessment} was applied to the full
repeat campaign of material class~A with the \SI{100}{\milli\meter} punch. The
data set contains the seven Nakazima widths from \texttt{W020} to \texttt{W200}
with two repetitions of each width, which gives fourteen DIC samples. Unless
stated otherwise all runs use $W_X=\SI{2}{\milli\meter}$,
$\mathrm{SAC}=\SI{5e-4}{\per\milli\meter}$, $n=8$, and $\alpha=0.1$ so that
$\Delta=\alpha\,\mathrm{SAC}=\SI{5e-5}{\per\milli\meter}$, together with the
time-based reference choice $M=0.8$.

The sensitivity to the matrix length $W_Y$ was studied first on the two
reference specimens, the narrow \texttt{W020} sample and the wide \texttt{W200}
sample. Tables~\ref{tab:minstoughton_w020_wy_sweep}
and~\ref{tab:minstoughton_w200_wy_sweep} report this sweep. For both samples the
setting $W_Y=\SI{15}{\milli\meter}$ gives the closest agreement with the
ISO~12004-2 average necking result, and larger matrix lengths lead to earlier
and lower onsets. The \texttt{W020} coverage decreases as $W_Y$ increases
because the requested matrix extends beyond the valid DIC field for the narrow
specimen. This value $W_Y=\SI{15}{\milli\meter}$ is therefore used for the
campaign analysis that follows.

The curvature criterion was then applied to all fourteen samples of the
campaign. Table~\ref{tab:minstoughton_campaign} reports the mean and the
standard deviation over the two repetitions of each width for the three
methods, and Figure~\ref{fig:flc_minstoughton_campaign} shows the resulting
forming limit points. The minor strain agrees across the three methods within
about \num{0.005} for every width up to \texttt{W120}, so all three methods
place each specimen at the same location on the diagram. The major limit strain
of the curvature criterion stays within a mean absolute difference of about
\num{0.02} of both reference methods over the campaign. The curvature criterion
reads slightly low in the plane-strain trough at \texttt{W080} and \texttt{W090}
and slightly high on the biaxial side at \texttt{W100}, where both repetitions
exceed the ISO~12004-2 and Volk--Hora values. On the biaxial \texttt{W200}
sample the three methods agree in major strain, while the curvature minor strain
falls about \num{0.03} below the two reference methods.

\section{Task III: Numerical Framework Verification Status}
\label{sec:numerical_results}

This section reports the verification status of the numerical framework rather
than a completed numerical FLC campaign. The surrogate material calibration
discussed in Chapter~\ref{chap:numerical_model}
means that the runs are interpreted as framework-verification cases rather than
quantitative predictions for the AA5754 experimental campaign. For each selected
configuration, the
pipeline creates the \software{Abaqus} model, submits the solver job on the
Euler cluster, runs the ODB-based post-processing, and synchronises a structured
set of result artefacts. The same interface is used for the Nakazima,
Marciniak, and Punch-in-Punch configurations, so that specimen width, sheet
thickness, punch radius, punch travel, material orientation, mass scaling, and
mesh refinement can be varied without changing the post-processing schema. Each
completed simulation is written to the same set of data products
(\texttt{punch\_fd.csv}, \texttt{energy\_data.csv},
\texttt{strain\_path.csv}, \texttt{forming\_limits.csv}, diagnostic figures,
and movies). This common structure is the basis for later comparison with the
Task~I experiments and for transfer to the Task~II identification framework.
The architecture itself was described in Chapter~\ref{chap:numerical_model} and
is not repeated here.

\subsection{Configuration Traceability}

Since the numerical framework is highly parameterised, each future numerical
result must be reported together with the configuration that generated it. The
full inventory of adjustable settings is given in
Appendix~\ref{app:configuration_parameters}. For a completed sensitivity or FLC
comparison, the reported configuration should include at least the specimen
geometry, sheet thickness, punch diameter, material orientation, punch-motion
profile, mesh-refinement factor, mass-scaling target, and selected
post-processing options. This preserves traceability without repeating the full
framework description in the results chapter.

\subsection{Quasi-Static Validation}
\label{sec:quasi_static_validation}

The simulations are carried out using \software{Abaqus/Explicit} and must
therefore be checked for quasi-static behaviour. For this purpose, the ratio
between kinetic energy and internal energy,
$\mathrm{ALLKE}/\mathrm{ALLIE}$, is monitored throughout the forming step. A
ratio below \SI{5}{\percent} is used as the acceptance criterion for the
planned sensitivity sweeps.

The way the punch motion is ramped over time also influences the inertial
response, and two velocity profiles were implemented for comparison. In the
smooth-step profile the punch velocity rises gradually from zero to a peak at
mid-stroke and returns to zero, whereas in the constant-velocity profile the
punch advances at a steady speed with only short smooth ramps at the start and
end of the stroke. The choice between them is a trade-off. The smooth-step
profile is numerically conservative because its gradual acceleration and
deceleration reduce inertial disturbances. The constant-velocity profile is
closer to the physical test bench because it reproduces steady punch advance
during forming, but it relies on the end ramps to avoid an inertial spike at
first punch contact. In both cases a gradual onset is essential, as an
instantaneous velocity step at first contact would increase the kinetic energy
and violate the quasi-static assumption.

\subsection{Sensitivity Study}
\label{sec:sensitivity_study}

The current sensitivity study is designed to determine suitable numerical
parameters for the production simulations. The punch velocity is held fixed at
\SI{5}{\milli\meter\per\second}, as established alongside the quasi-static
solution strategy in Chapter~\ref{chap:numerical_model}, so that one degree of
freedom is fixed and the two remaining numerical controls can be studied
separately: the mass-scaling target time increment and the mesh-refinement
level. The runs use the Nakazima \texttt{W100} specimen with the \SI{100}{\milli\meter}
punch as the scalar reference case.

\subsubsection{Mass Scaling Analysis}
\label{sec:mass_scaling_analysis}

The mass-scaling sweep varies the target time increment while keeping the mesh
refinement factor fixed at $f_\mathrm{MR}=2$. The evaluated target increments are
$1\times10^{-6}$, $5\times10^{-6}$, $1\times10^{-5}$, $5\times10^{-5}$, and
$1\times10^{-4}$\,\si{\second}. The comparison is based on the
$\mathrm{ALLKE}/\mathrm{ALLIE}$ ratio and on the extracted forming-limit point.
Increasing the target time increment reduces the computational cost, but also
increases the risk of inertial effects. The final value will therefore be
chosen as the largest time increment that still satisfies the quasi-static
energy criterion without changing the failure strain appreciably.

Figure~\ref{fig:ms_sweep_results} shows that this criterion is restrictive for
the present \texttt{W100} reference case. The full-range energy plot gives the overall
magnitude of the inertial response, while the \SIrange{0}{10}{\percent} zoom
resolves the neighbourhood of the \SI{5}{\percent} criterion. After the initial
contact transient is omitted, only
$\Delta t_\mathrm{MS}=\SI{1e-6}{\second}$ remains below the
\SI{5}{\percent} $\mathrm{ALLKE}/\mathrm{ALLIE}$ limit. The larger target time
increments produce visibly similar strain paths over much of the process,
but their energy ratios are too high for a quasi-static production setting.

\subsubsection{Mesh Refinement Analysis}
\label{sec:mesh_refinement_analysis}

The mesh-refinement sweep varies the global mesh-refinement factor while keeping
the mass-scaling target fixed at
$\Delta t_\mathrm{MS}=\SI{1e-5}{\second}$. The
evaluated refinement factors are $1$, $2$, and $3$. The meshes are generated
automatically by sweeping the global refinement factor of
Section~\ref{sec:mesh_generation}, which scales all section sizes together while
leaving the sectioning unchanged (Figure~\ref{fig:mesh_mr_sweep}). Since strain
localisation is mesh-sensitive, the production mesh will be selected as the
coarsest discretisation for which the extracted forming-limit point has
converged, that is, the largest element size beyond which further refinement no
longer changes the failure strain appreciably.

The strain paths in Figure~\ref{fig:mr_sweep_results} are close over most of
the deformation history, indicating that the scalar \texttt{W100} response is not
strongly affected by the tested mesh-refinement factors before the final
localisation stage. The accompanying energy overview and

$\Delta t_\mathrm{MS}=\SI{1e-5}{\second}$ target is not yet an acceptable
quasi-static setting for this mesh study; increasing $f_\mathrm{MR}$ reduces the
energy ratio, but the curves remain above the \SI{5}{\percent} limit over part
of the simulation. A production mesh choice should therefore be confirmed with a
mesh-refinement sweep repeated at the accepted mass-scaling target.

\subsubsection{Selection of Numerical Parameters}
\label{sec:selection_numerical_parameters}

The final production settings will be selected by combining the mass-scaling
and mesh-refinement sweeps. The target is the least computationally expensive
configuration that satisfies both the quasi-static energy criterion and the
strain-convergence criterion.

For the completed \texttt{W100} scalar runs, the mass-scaling sweep identifies
$\Delta t_\mathrm{MS}=\SI{1e-6}{\second}$ as the only tested target satisfying
the \SI{5}{\percent} energy criterion. The mesh-refinement sweep at
$\Delta t_\mathrm{MS}=\SI{1e-5}{\second}$ is useful as a discretisation
diagnostic, but it should not be used alone to select production settings
because the quasi-static criterion is violated. The next confirmation sweep
should therefore repeat the mesh-refinement comparison at
$\Delta t_\mathrm{MS}=\SI{1e-6}{\second}$, preferably for
$f_\mathrm{MR}=1$, $2$, and $4$, so that the selected production mesh is judged
under the same quasi-static setting that will be used for the final simulations.

nearly coincident up to the late forming stage for
$\mu_\mathrm{punch}=0.10$--0.25. The main difference is therefore not a large
change in the global force level, but a shift in the localisation and extracted
failure point.

The friction check therefore supports two conclusions. First, using a very low
punch--blank friction coefficient in production simulations would be
questionable, because the $\mu_\mathrm{punch}=0.05$ case changes both the
strain path and the extracted failure travel. Second, the available \texttt{W120} sweep
is not sufficient to identify an
optimal coefficient: the intermediate and high-friction cases have similar
force histories, the fracture-strain trend is not monotonic, and all five runs
remain above the \SI{5}{\percent} quasi-static energy criterion. A defensible
friction choice should therefore be calibrated against the experimental
force--displacement response and then rechecked using the final accepted
mass-scaling and mesh-refinement settings.

\subsection{Current Numerical Output Status}
\label{sec:numerical_output_status}

At the present report stage, the numerical runs are used to verify the pipeline
rather than to construct final numerical forming-limit curves. The verified
outputs are the automated model generation, cluster submission,
post-processing file structure, energy-history extraction, force--displacement
export, strain-path export, and diagnostic visualisation output. These checks
show that the framework can generate the data required for a numerical FLC
campaign, but they do not yet establish a validated numerical forming limit for
the Nakazima, Marciniak, or Punch-in-Punch configurations.

A final numerical results section would require three additional items: a
completed mass-scaling sweep satisfying the quasi-static energy criterion, a
completed mesh-refinement sweep demonstrating convergence of the extracted
failure strain, and production simulations across the relevant specimen widths.
Only after these steps can the numerical forming-limit points be compared
directly with the experimental FLCs from Task~I.
