\chapter{FLC Identification Framework}
\label{chap:identification_framework}

\section{Motivation and Scope}

The identification of forming-limit points is a key step in the construction
of forming limit curves. After a forming test, the measured strain field must
be evaluated in order to determine the strain state at the onset of localized
necking. Since different identification criteria can lead to different limit
strains, it is useful to compare them within the same post-processing workflow.

In this project, the starting point was an existing graphical user interface
developed in the laboratory for the post-processing of DIC measurements. This
tool already included data import, visualization, plotting routines, and the
DIN/ISO~12004-2 and Volk–Hora identification methods. The contribution of the
present work was therefore to study an additional criterion based on the work
of Min, Stoughton, Carsley, and Lin, and to prepare a draft methodology for its
future integration into the existing framework.

\section{Existing Identification Criteria}

The existing framework contains two forming-limit identification methods. The
DIN/ISO~12004-2 method evaluates the strain distribution along a section
crossing the localization or crack region and determines the limit strain from
a spatial fit. The Volk–Hora criterion uses the temporal evolution of the
strain field to detect the onset of instability.
Both methods are available within the same GUI environment, which allows them
to be applied to the same DIC dataset.

\section{Draft of the Min–Stoughton Criterion}

The Min–Stoughton criterion was studied as a possible extension of the
existing FLC identification framework. The method of Min, Stoughton, Carsley
and Lin~\cite{Min2017} is a spatio-temporal curvature criterion: instead of
identifying necking from a final strain profile, it tracks the evolution of the
measured three-dimensional specimen surface and detects the sudden curvature
increase caused by the first local through-thickness dimple. This links the
criterion directly to the physical picture of localized necking: after diffuse
necking, a small surface dimple appears and then expands into the final neck
band.

In the improved three-dimensional version of the method, the curvature is not
fitted along a single manually selected line. A narrow rectangular point matrix
is placed around the necking region, with width $W_X$ across the neck band and
length $W_Y$ along the neck band. For Marciniak tests, the DIC surface
coordinates $(X,Y,Z)$ are extracted for this matrix over the image sequence. A
reference frame $M$ is selected before localized necking, and the background
surface shape is removed by subtracting the reference height,
$Z'_{ij}(k)=Z_{ij}(k)-Z_{ij}(M)$. A small superimposed artificial curvature
($C_\mathrm{SAC}$) is then added before fitting circles to the profiles in the
matrix columns; this stabilizes the least-squares circle fit when the true
curvature is close to zero. The curvature signal $C_\mathrm{pm}(k)$ is defined
as the averaged fitted curvature over the point matrix.

The onset frame is determined from the time history of $C_\mathrm{pm}$. For a
candidate frame $K$, a predicted curvature $C_{\mathrm{pm},P}$ is obtained by a
linear regression through the previously measured curvature values. The onset
of localized necking is assigned to the first frame $K$ for which
\[
  C_\mathrm{pm}(k) > C_{\mathrm{pm},P}(k) + \Delta
  \quad \text{for} \quad K \leq k \leq K+n-1 .
\]
In the examples of Min et al.~\cite{Min2017}, $n=8$ consecutive frames and
$\Delta=C_\mathrm{SAC}/10$ were used. The parameters $W_X$, $W_Y$,
$C_\mathrm{SAC}$, $n$, and the reference-frame selection therefore form the
practical calibration set of the method. The paper suggests
$2\,\mathrm{mm}\leq W_X\leq 8\,\mathrm{mm}$ and
$10\,\mathrm{mm}<W_Y\leq 40\,\mathrm{mm}$ for the investigated cases, with
smaller $W_X$ suitable for neck bands perpendicular to the major stress
direction and larger $W_X$ for inclined or crossed neck bands.

For Nakazima tests, the same physical curvature idea cannot be applied directly
to the raw $(Y,Z)$ profiles because the specimen undergoes out-of-plane
wrapping over a hemispherical punch. Min et al.~\cite{Min2017} therefore
transform the measured surface into an equivalent profile described by the
spherical arc distance $D$ and the distance $R_\mathrm{out}$ from the punch
centre. The reference subtraction is then performed on $R_\mathrm{out}$ rather
than on $Z$,
$R'_{\mathrm{out},ij}(k)=R_{\mathrm{out},ij}(k)-R_{\mathrm{out},ij}(M)$, and
the same curvature-history criterion is applied. This transformation requires
the punch radius, sheet thickness, pole position, strain state at the pole, and
the deformed DIC surface coordinates. It also imposes an important validity
condition: the reference frame has to be selected after the evaluated local
surface has wrapped onto the punch but before localized necking starts.

Within this project the Min–Stoughton method was therefore treated as a
methodology and data-integration task rather than as a finalized GUI feature.
The required VIC3D quantities are the measured surface positions and
displacements $(X,Y,Z,U,V,W)$, the strain fields $(\varepsilon_1,
\varepsilon_2)$, the crack or localization location used to place the point
matrix, and metadata such as punch diameter and sheet thickness. A provisional
standalone workflow was created to test these data links and to explore the
Nakazima transformation, but the detected onset remained sensitive to the
matrix dimensions, reference-frame choice, smoothing, and persistence
definition. The final integration therefore still requires a validated
parameter choice and a consistent treatment of decimated VIC3D frame sequences.
